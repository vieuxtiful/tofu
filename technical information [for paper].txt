Yes. The two developments fit into a single probabilistic vision architecture:

- **Cicerone reasons discriminatively:** “Which glyph identities remain possible, and which can I eliminate?”
- **Scene reasons generatively/counterfactually:** “What physical surface and inscription process could have produced these surviving pixels?”
- **Fusion answers:** “Given both the visible evidence and the world that produced it, what was most probably written here?”

The crucial safety constraint is that neither layer should manufacture certainty when the glyph evidence has been destroyed.

# Proposed program: ToFU Vision 2

## 1. Unifying theoretical model

Treat an observed text region \(X\) as the result of a latent physical process:

\[
X = \mathcal I\big(\mathcal P(G,S,M),D,L,V\big)+\epsilon
\]

where:

- \(G\): original glyph or GT string;
- \(S\): typographic style—font, weight, spacing, orientation;
- \(M\): material—painted metal, paper, glass, fabric, brick, plastic, wood, emissive display, etc.;
- \(\mathcal P\): inscription process—paint, print, engraving, embossing, vinyl, chalk, illumination;
- \(D\): physical degradation—abrasion, fading, corrosion, peeling, occlusion;
- \(L\): illumination and reflection;
- \(V\): camera geometry, perspective, blur and resolution;
- \(\epsilon\): sensor/compression noise.

The intended posterior is:

\[
P(G\mid X,\mathrm{GT})
\propto
P_{\text{Cicerone}}(G\mid X,\mathrm{GT})
\,
P_{\text{Scene}}(X\mid G,M,S,\mathcal P,D,L,V)
\,
P(G\mid\mathrm{GT},\text{language}).
\]

This formulation keeps the responsibilities clean:

- Cicerone estimates a discriminative posterior.
- Scene estimates whether a proposed glyph could plausibly have generated the observation under the inferred world.
- GT and language supply constrained priors, not answers imposed regardless of pixels.

# Track A: Cicerone — degradation-aware glyph identity

## Objective

Teach Cicerone to recover identity-preserving features from degraded glyphs and eliminate visually incompatible GT candidates.

Its reasoning sequence becomes:

```text
Observed pixels
  → degradation-robust features
  → incompatible glyphs eliminated
  → surviving identities ranked
  → calibrated match or abstention
```

This is “what the glyph is not” leading to “what the glyph is.”

## A1. Canonical glyph encoder

Create a shared encoder \(E_\theta\) for:

- observed scene glyphs;
- canonical GT renderings;
- synthetically degraded versions of those renderings.

Canonical class masks are well supported by recent scene-text research: class-aware mask guidance was reported to suppress background/style noise and improve challenging-dataset recognition by an average 4.1% in CAM. [Class-Aware Mask-Guided Feature Refinement](https://arxiv.org/abs/2402.13643)

Training positives:

\[
(E_\theta(R(G)), E_\theta(D(R(G))))
\]

Training hard negatives:

\[
(E_\theta(D(R(G_i))),E_\theta(R(G_j))),\quad G_i\neq G_j
\]

with negative sampling concentrated on:

- Unicode confusables;
- OCR confusion history;
- same-radical CJK characters;
- accent and detached-mark variants;
- `0/O`, `1/I/l`, `5/S`, `8/B`;
- font-dependent near-homoglyphs.

## A2. Combined loss

\[
\mathcal L_C =
\mathcal L_{\mathrm{NLL}}
+\lambda_1\mathcal L_{\mathrm{contrastive}}
+\lambda_2\mathcal L_{\mathrm{hard-negative}}
+\lambda_3\mathcal L_{\mathrm{consistency}}
+\lambda_4\mathcal L_{\mathrm{OT}}
+\lambda_5\mathcal L_{\mathrm{calibration}}.
\]

Components:

- **Negative log-likelihood:** makes the correct GT match probable.
- **Contrastive loss:** makes degradation variants invariant while separating identities.
- **Hard-negative loss:** explicitly attacks known confusions.
- **Consistency loss:** requires blur, compression and partial occlusion variants to produce stable predictions.
- **Entropy-regularized optimal transport:** softly aligns observed components or feature patches with GT glyph components.
- **Calibration loss:** prevents high scores from becoming synonymous with correctness.

The OT term is especially useful when one-to-one connected-component alignment fails:

\[
\mathcal L_{\mathrm{OT}}
=
\min_{\pi\in\Pi}
\langle \pi,C\rangle
+
\varepsilon\sum_{ij}\pi_{ij}\log\pi_{ij}.
\]

## A3. Explicit negative evidence

Cicerone should record both support and contradiction:

```json
{
  "candidate": "嶽",
  "support": {
    "global_shape": 0.81,
    "component_alignment": 0.76,
    "ocr_agreement": 0.52
  },
  "contradictions": {
    "missing_discriminating_component": 0.14,
    "incompatible_left_radical": 0.03
  },
  "match_probability": 0.74,
  "evidence_survival": 0.61
}
```

A candidate may win because alternatives are contradicted, but it cannot be green unless sufficient positive evidence survives.

## A4. Evidence-survival estimate

Introduce:

\[
q_{\text{visible}}=P(\text{identity-bearing evidence survives}\mid X).
\]

This separates two questions:

1. Which candidate scores highest?
2. Is enough information present to justify choosing any candidate?

If \(q_{\text{visible}}\) is low, the result remains indeterminate even if one GT term ranks first.

# Track B: Scene — material-conditioned glyph reconstruction

## Objective

Upgrade Scene from surface detection and appearance description into a physical-material inference layer:

```text
Surface pixels
  → material + inscription process
  → degradation and imaging model
  → counterfactual clean glyph hypotheses
  → likelihood of each GT candidate
```

This is “what the glyph was in this world” leading to “what the glyph is.”

Material recognition is a legitimate contextual signal: MINC demonstrated large-scale material classification and per-pixel material segmentation in unconstrained images, while geometry-informed recognition showed that surface geometry improves material classification across scales and viewpoints. [MINC](https://openaccess.thecvf.com/content_cvpr_2015/html/Bell_Material_Recognition_in_2015_CVPR_paper.html), [Geometry-Informed Material Recognition](https://openaccess.thecvf.com/content_cvpr_2016/html/DeGol_Geometry-Informed_Material_Recognition_CVPR_2016_paper.html)

## B1. Hierarchical material taxonomy

Scene should predict a distribution, not one brittle label:

```text
surface
├── rigid
│   ├── metal
│   │   ├── painted
│   │   ├── corroded
│   │   └── reflective
│   ├── stone/masonry
│   ├── glass
│   └── plastic
├── fibrous
│   ├── paper
│   ├── fabric
│   └── wood
└── emissive
    ├── LED
    ├── LCD
    └── neon/backlit
```

Predict associated traits:

- roughness;
- reflectance/specularity;
- porosity;
- texture frequency;
- surface normal/perspective;
- periodicity;
- translucency;
- deformation;
- likely inscription process.

A hierarchical output allows Scene to remain useful when it knows “rough masonry” but cannot distinguish brick from concrete.

## B2. Inscription-process model

Material alone is insufficient. Scene should infer how the glyph was applied:

- printed;
- painted;
- sprayed/stenciled;
- engraved;
- embossed;
- etched;
- vinyl/appliqué;
- chalked;
- illuminated;
- displayed electronically.

Each process constrains plausible degradation:

| Process | Likely degradation |
|---|---|
| Paint on metal | fading, rust breakthrough, peeling |
| Ink on paper | bleed, wrinkles, stains, fiber absorption |
| Engraving | shadows, highlights, relief, occlusion |
| Vinyl | lifting edges, tears, specular reflections |
| Chalk | granular loss, smearing, low contrast |
| LED/display | bloom, scan patterns, saturation, moiré |
| Paint on brick | discontinuity across mortar and roughness |

## B3. Learn a surface-conditioned forward model

Given a clean glyph \(G\), material \(M\), process \(\mathcal P\), and degradation code \(z_D\):

\[
\hat X =
F_\phi(G,M,\mathcal P,z_D,L,V).
\]

This model predicts how a candidate would appear on the observed surface. Scene then compares \(\hat X\) with the actual crop:

\[
S_{\text{world}}(G)
=
-\operatorname{Dist}(\hat X_G,X).
\]

The distance should combine:

- perceptual feature distance;
- edge/stroke agreement;
- local texture residual;
- color/reflectance agreement;
- glyph-mask overlap;
- frequency-domain agreement.

This is safer than asking an unconstrained restoration model to output one clean image: Scene evaluates candidate-specific counterfactuals.

## B4. Degradation-code inference

Infer:

\[
z_D = H_\psi(X,\text{surrounding surface}).
\]

The surrounding non-glyph surface is important because it reveals:

- blur and compression shared by the image;
- material texture underneath the inscription;
- lighting direction;
- specularity;
- corrosion or weathering;
- repeating structural patterns.

Recent restoration work supports explicitly separating degradation information from content information rather than treating restoration as an undifferentiated mapping. [Improving Image Restoration through Removing Degradations in Textual Representations](https://openaccess.thecvf.com/content/CVPR2024/papers/Lin_Improving_Image_Restoration_through_Removing_Degradations_in_Textual_Representations_CVPR_2024_paper.pdf)

## B5. Restoration is evidence, not source truth

Any restored visualization must carry:

- restoration method;
- material hypothesis;
- candidate that conditioned it;
- uncertainty;
- changed-pixel mask;
- whether the discriminating feature was observed or synthesized.

A diffusion or super-resolution model must not silently feed hallucinated strokes back as observed evidence. The verifier should compare candidate-generated observations with the original image, not OCR an unconstrained “cleaned” image and treat that output as fact.

# Track C: Cicerone–Scene fusion

## C1. New evidence contract

Add a structured, optional evidence object without changing the layer boundary:

```python
MaterialEvidence:
    class_distribution
    traits
    inscription_process
    geometry
    degradation_code
    confidence
    provenance

GlyphMatchEvidence:
    gt_candidate
    discriminative_score
    counterfactual_score
    contradiction_score
    evidence_survival
    calibration
    decision
```

These objects should live inside the manifest’s per-instance evidence/provenance fields.

## C2. Bidirectional execution

ToFU already runs Scene before and after Cicerone. Extend that pattern:

```mermaid
flowchart LR
    A["Scene pre-pass"] --> B["Surface/material proposals"]
    B --> C["Cicerone detection and OCR"]
    C --> D["Degradation-aware GT matcher"]
    D --> E["Scene counterfactual evaluator"]
    E --> F["Cicerone candidate reranking"]
    F --> G["Verify calibration and abstention"]
```

Avoid an unbounded feedback loop. Permit exactly one structured reconsideration pass initially.

## C3. Posterior fusion

For GT candidate \(g\):

\[
\log S(g)=
w_c\log P_C(g\mid X)
+w_s\log P_S(X\mid g,M,\mathcal P,D)
+w_l\log P_{\text{lang}}(g)
+w_o\log P_{\text{OCR}}(g)
-w_nC(g).
\]

Here \(C(g)\) is accumulated contradiction evidence.

Weights should be calibrated by script, material and degradation stratum. They should not be manually inferred from Word2World’s social-modality weights.

## C4. Decision policy

- **Green:** high calibrated posterior, sufficient evidence survival, no serious contradiction, and stable across degradation/material hypotheses.
- **Amber:** one leading candidate, but low surviving evidence or material ambiguity.
- **Red:** candidates inconsistent with pixels or no credible surface model.
- **Indeterminate:** observation is information-theoretically inadequate.

# Development roadmap

## Phase 0 — Measurement foundation

Duration: 2–3 weeks.

- Extend fixture annotations with material, process and degradation labels.
- Define paired clean/degraded glyph examples.
- Record whether identity-bearing features remain genuinely visible.
- Freeze train/validation/test assets by scene, not crop, to prevent leakage.
- Establish Cicerone-only and current Scene baselines.

Exit gate: inter-annotator agreement and a reproducible degradation-stratified benchmark.

## Phase 1 — Cicerone degradation matcher

Duration: 4–6 weeks.

- Build canonical GT renderer and degradation generator.
- Train a small Euclidean Siamese glyph encoder.
- Add hard-negative mining from current OCR correction history.
- Add calibrated match, contradiction and evidence-survival outputs.
- Integrate only as an amber recommendation.

Exit gates:

- ≥8-point exact-match improvement on risky GT-covered regions;
- ≥98% green precision;
- expected calibration error ≤0.05;
- no script stratum regresses by more than 2 points.

## Phase 2 — Entropic component alignment

Duration: 3–4 weeks.

- Extract patch/component embeddings.
- Add Sinkhorn/entropy-regularized OT.
- Test split, merged, occluded and detached-mark glyphs.
- Compare against Savor’s deterministic alignment.

Exit gate: significant gain on segmentation-damaged strata without increasing false substitutions.

## Phase 3 — Scene material intelligence

Duration: 5–8 weeks.

- Add hierarchical material and trait classification.
- Predict inscription process and degradation family.
- Use context outside the glyph mask.
- Store uncertainty and provenance in `TextManifest`.
- Keep all new signals observational; no restoration yet.

Exit gates:

- useful top-level material accuracy;
- calibrated material probabilities;
- measurable improvement in downstream glyph matching when material evidence is supplied.

## Phase 4 — Surface-conditioned forward rendering

Duration: 6–10 weeks.

- Train or implement candidate-conditioned degradation simulation.
- Generate \(\hat X_G\) for top-\(k\) GT candidates.
- Score candidates against original pixels.
- Add material-marginalized likelihood:

\[
P(X\mid G)=\sum_m P(X\mid G,m)P(m\mid X).
\]

Exit gate: candidate-conditioned counterfactual scores improve ranking over the Cicerone-only arm.

## Phase 5 — Controlled fusion

Duration: 3–5 weeks.

Run four preregistered arms:

1. current ToFU;
2. Cicerone degradation matcher;
3. Cicerone + material descriptors;
4. Cicerone + full Scene counterfactual scoring.

Hyperbolic embeddings should be a fifth ablation, not part of the baseline.

## Phase 6 — Green automation

Only after held-out evidence:

- allow automatic GT correction for strongly calibrated cases;
- retain amber review for destructive ambiguity;
- expose why candidates were eliminated;
- monitor post-deployment corrections by material/script/degradation.

# Research hypotheses

### Cicerone hypothesis

\[
H_{0C}:
\]

Degradation-aware contrastive matching does not improve exact GT identification after controlling for script, resolution and OCR confidence.

\[
H_{1C}:
\]

It improves risky-region exact identification by at least 8 absolute points while maintaining green precision ≥98%.

### Scene hypothesis

\[
H_{0S}:
\]

Material/process information provides no additional predictive value after conditioning on crop pixels and OCR evidence.

\[
H_{1S}:
\]

Material-conditioned counterfactual scoring improves top-1 GT ranking by at least 5 points on physically degraded text and reduces confident confusions without increasing abstention by more than 10%.

### Fusion hypothesis

\[
H_{1F}:
\]

Cicerone and Scene provide complementary information: the combined model significantly outperforms either layer alone, particularly for abrasion, reflectance, substrate discontinuity and partial occlusion.

# Recommended priority

1. **Cicerone degradation-invariant matcher:** highest feasibility and clearest evaluation.
2. **Evidence-survival/calibration model:** essential safety mechanism.
3. **Scene material and process taxonomy:** creates world context without hallucination.
4. **Entropic component alignment:** targeted solution for damaged segmentation.
5. **Candidate-conditioned forward restoration:** high potential, higher research risk.
6. **Hyperbolic representation:** retain only if an ablation proves that the script/glyph/material hierarchy benefits retrieval.

The eventual conceptual identity is strong:

> **Cicerone reconstructs identity from surviving evidence. Scene reconstructs the physical process that produced the evidence. Verify decides whether their agreement deserves trust.**
